Private-Label Purchasing
at Kroger

An analysis of household income and private-label spending, examining overall purchasing patterns and differences within Grocery categories.

Download R script

Business Question

Do lower-income Kroger households allocate a larger share of their spending to private-label products than higher-income households, and is that difference concentrated in Grocery categories?

Unit of Analysis

Our unit of analysis is the household.

We use households because income is measured at the household level. Each household therefore contributes one summarized private-label purchasing measure rather than households with more transactions receiving more weight.

Defining Private-Label Purchasing

We define private-label purchasing as private-label dollar share:

\[ \text{Private Label Share} = \frac{\text{Private Label Spending}} {\text{Total Spending}} \]

This measures the share of a household’s spending that goes to private-label products.

ANOVA

Welch’s one way ANOVA best because of unequal sizes and different standard deviations
View R code 
oneway.test(
    private_share ~ income_group,
    data = household_data,
    var.equal = FALSE
)

Welch ANOVA Result:

  • F = 5.7469
  • p = 0.003509

Average private-label dollar share is not the same across all three income groups.

Your grouped means are:

  • Below $50K: 28.9%
  • $50K–$99K: 27.2%
  • $100K+: 25.0%

So broad pattern is: Private-label dollar share decreases as household income rises.

ANOVA only tells shows at least one group differs, not which pairs differ.

Next step is a pairwise follow-up with Welch pairwise t-tests with a multiple-comparison adjustment
View R code 
pairwise.t.test(
    household_data$private_share,
    household_data$income_group,
    p.adjust.method = "bonferroni",
    pool.sd = FALSE
)

Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise p-values:

  • $50K–$99K vs $100K+: p = 0.256
  • Below $50K vs $100K+: p = 0.004
  • Below $50K vs $50K–$99K: p = 0.173

Only statistically significant pair Below $50K vs $100K+

And the observed mean difference is 0.039 (0.289 - 0.250)

Households below $50K spend about 3.9 percentage points more of their total spending on private-label products than households earning $100K+. The middle-income group is not significantly different from either side after the Bonferroni adjustment.

Private-label dollar share differs across income groups overall, but the clearest difference is between households below $50K and households earning $100K or more.

Quantify key difference with confidence interval
View R code 
t.test(
    private_share ~ income_group,
    data = household_data |>
        filter(income_group %in% c("Below $50K", "$100K+"))
)
  • $100K+ mean PL share: 25.05%
  • Below $50K mean PL share: 28.88%
  • Difference: about 3.83 percentage points
  • 95% CI for $100K+ − Below $50K: -6.15 to -1.51 percentage points
  • p = 0.00133

Households earning below $50K spend about 3.8 percentage points more of their total dollars on private label products than households earning $100K or more. The 95% confidence interval suggests the true gap is roughly 1.5 to 6.2 percentage points.

Test whether those category gaps are statistically distinguishable.
View R code 
commodity_tests <- commodity_data |>
    group_by(COMMODITY_DESC) |>
    group_modify(~ {
        test <- t.test(
            private_share ~ income_group,
            data = .x
        )

        tibble(
            difference = unname(
                test$estimate["mean in group Below $50K"] -
                test$estimate["mean in group $100K+"]
            ),
            conf_low = -test$conf.int[2],
            conf_high = -test$conf.int[1],
            p_value = test$p.value
        )
    }) |>
    ungroup() |>
    mutate(
        p_adjusted = p.adjust(p_value, method = "bonferroni")
    )

Lower-income households allocate a larger share of Grocery spending to private label, with particularly pronounced differences in soft drinks, cheese, and fluid milk.

Recommendation

Kroger should prioritize private-label positioning and promotion for lower-income households in high-volume Grocery categories where the income gap in private-label purchasing is strongest.

The clearest opportunities in this analysis are:

  • Cheese
  • Soft drinks
  • Fluid milk products

Kroger should also investigate why $100K+ households show lower private-label adoption in these categories.

Limitations

  • Income is only available for a subset of households.
  • This is observational analysis, so income should not be interpreted as causing private-label purchasing.
  • Household composition, shopping frequency, store location, and assortment could also contribute to the observed differences.
  • The commodity analysis examines only five high-sales Grocery categories.

Conclusion

Private-label dollar share differs across income groups. The clearest difference is between households below $50K and households earning $100K or more.

That gap becomes larger within Grocery and is most evident among the selected high-volume categories in cheese, soft drinks, and fluid milk products.
